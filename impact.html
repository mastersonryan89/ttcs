Where we are

We would rather show our method than a number we cannot source

Six programs are running today at 1511 S. High St. Two more launch in 2026. We are a young organization, and our outcome data is still being collected under a defined method rather than assembled after the fact.

Plenty of organizations our size publish a big round figure with nothing behind it. We are not going to do that. Every number we eventually put on this page will have a definition, a time period and a source we can hand to a funder on request.

In the meantime, what we can show you is the work itself — who we serve, what we track and how we define success. If you want to see it in person, come to the office.

What we measure

The six things we track

Chosen because they are the things that actually predict whether someone stays out, stays working and stays housed — not because they are easy to count.

Program completion

How many people who start a program finish it. A low completion rate tells us the program is wrong, not that the participants are.

Employment placement

How many participants start a job, and what kind of job it is. A placement into work somebody can live on counts differently from any placement at all.

Job retention at 90 days

The one that matters most. Getting hired is not the outcome — still being there three months later is, because that is when a paycheck becomes a life.

Housing stability

Not just whether somebody got housed, but whether they are still housed six months on. Placement without stability is a statistic, not a result.

Recidivism

Whether the people we work with return to jail or prison. It is the hardest number to collect honestly and the most important one to a funder.

Return and referral

How many people come back for a second service, and how many arrive because someone who used us sent them. Trust shows up in the numbers before anything else does.

How we count

The definitions we hold ourselves to

Written down in advance, so we cannot move the goalposts once the results are in.

Rule one

A person is counted once

Somebody who uses three of our programs is one individual served, not three. Counting services as people is the most common way small nonprofits inflate a number without technically lying.

Rule two

The period is always stated

Every figure carries the date range it covers. A number without a period attached can mean anything, which is another way of saying it means nothing.

Rule three

Outcomes beat outputs

Classes delivered and hours provided are outputs. Jobs held at ninety days and homes kept at six months are outcomes. We report both, and we do not confuse them.

Rule four

People who leave still count

Participants who drop out stay in the denominator. A completion rate calculated only from the people who finished is not a completion rate.

What we see

What the work looks like from inside it

No figures attached to any of this. These are the patterns we run into week after week, and they shape how the programs are built.

Transportation ends more placements than attitude

The job is across town, the shift starts before the bus does, and by week three it is over. This is why bus passes and gas cards sit at the top of our needs list.

Paperwork blocks everything behind it

No ID means no job, no lease and no benefits. A twenty-dollar document is regularly the only thing standing between somebody and the rest of their plan.

Needs arrive in bundles

Almost nobody walks in with one problem. Housing, work and recovery show up together, which is why a program that only solves one of them tends not to hold.

The first ninety days decide it

Most of what goes wrong goes wrong early. That is why our support does not stop at the offer letter or the move-in date.

Lived experience opens the door faster

People tell the truth sooner to someone who has been where they are. It is the reason our support groups are peer-led.

Coming back is a good sign

Somebody who returns after something went wrong is somebody who believes the door is still open. We treat that as progress, not failure.

Stories

The part numbers leave out

Participant stories will appear here — in their own words, with their written permission, and never before.

Why this space is empty

Our participants are in reentry and recovery. Publishing someone's story without their explicit consent can cost them a job, a lease or a custody arrangement. Consent has to be written, specific and revocable, and it has to be given by someone who is not currently depending on us for housing or work.

When we have a story told that way, it goes here. Until then this section stays empty on purpose.
